The Ten Essentials

Ten small systems that help turn an unexpected delay, weather change, navigation error, or minor injury into a manageable problem.

Think in systems, not ten gadgets

The Ten Essentials are categories. Their contents change with the route, season, duration, forecast, remoteness, group, and individual needs. A city greenway and an exposed mountain route do not require identical equipment—but both deserve deliberate preparation.

Before packing: check official alerts and weather, leave a trip plan with someone who is not going, set a firm turnaround time, and choose a route within the group’s capabilities.

Pack, purpose, test

  1. Navigation

    Know where you are, where you are going, and how to retreat.

    Pack: current map, compass, route notes, and an offline map or GPS where useful.

    Test: locate yourself and identify a safe alternate route without cellular service.

  2. Sun protection

    Limit sunburn, glare, and heat load.

    Pack: sunglasses, sunscreen, protective clothing, and a brimmed hat appropriate to conditions.

    Test: confirm coverage, fit, expiration, and reapplication timing before exposure.

  3. Insulation

    Stay functional when temperature, wind, precipitation, or pace changes.

    Pack: an extra warm layer plus wind/rain protection; add gloves and warm headwear when conditions justify them.

    Test: can the layers go over what you are wearing, and are they still useful when damp?

  4. Illumination

    Move, work, and signal after darkness or in low visibility.

    Pack: headlamp or flashlight and compatible spare batteries or a verified charging plan.

    Test: switch it on, check remaining power, lock it against accidental activation, and reach it one-handed.

  5. First-aid supplies

    Manage likely minor injuries and support a larger emergency until help arrives.

    Pack: a trip-sized kit, personal medications, allergy response items, blister/skin supplies, and a concise reference.

    Test: know where everything is and replace used, damaged, wet, or expired supplies.

  6. Fire

    Create emergency heat or a signal only where conditions and regulations permit.

    Pack: a lighter or waterproof matches plus prepared fire starter; select alternatives when open flame is prohibited.

    Test: practice safely before the trip and check current fire restrictions. Never rely on starting a fire for survival.

  7. Repair kit and tools

    Restore critical equipment or improvise a safe workaround.

    Pack: knife or multi-tool, tape, cordage, and parts specific to footwear, shelter, pack, bike, or mobility equipment.

    Test: make one realistic repair with the exact kit you carry.

  8. Extra nutrition

    Maintain energy through a delay or route change.

    Pack: an extra day of no-cook, calorie-dense food that you tolerate and can open easily.

    Test: eat it on a practice outing and confirm it survives heat, cold, crushing, and dietary restrictions.

  9. Hydration

    Carry enough water and have a credible resupply/treatment plan.

    Pack: water sized to heat, exertion, duration, and individual needs, plus treatment when reliable refill sources exist.

    Test: measure actual consumption on a comparable outing and practice the treatment method.

  10. Emergency shelter

    Reduce exposure to wind, rain, cold, heat, and ground conditions during an unplanned stop.

    Pack: a bivy, tarp, emergency blanket, or other shelter you can deploy with your available mobility and dexterity.

    Test: set it up in daylight, then repeat with gloves or limited light.

Add the personal and adaptive layer

The standard systems are a baseline. Add anything required to keep a specific person safe and mobile:

  • Medication and medical equipment: prescribed rescue medication, dosing information, glucose supplies, inhaler, epinephrine, and device consumables.
  • Mobility support: prosthetic socks/liners, skin-care supplies, spare wheelchair or mobility-device parts, compact tools, patch materials, and a realistic power plan.
  • Communication and sensory access: hearing-aid batteries, glasses, communication cards, accessible alerts, whistle, and a satellite communicator or locator beacon when the route warrants it.
  • Load and route: distribute group equipment, preserve access to critical supplies, and choose surfaces, distance, elevation, and exit options that match the user.

For adaptive load placement, see the pack-load balance guide.

A 20-minute field test

  1. Pack all ten systems for a real upcoming route.
  2. Carry the pack for ten minutes and note pain, imbalance, inaccessible pockets, or excessive weight.
  3. Without using cellular data, identify your position and return route.
  4. Retrieve your light and first-aid supplies with one hand, then deploy your shelter.
  5. Record missing skills or supplies. Fix those gaps before adding convenience items.
Stop conditions matter more than equipment. Turn around for worsening weather, pain, fatigue, unstable equipment, route uncertainty, or a pace that threatens your return time.
