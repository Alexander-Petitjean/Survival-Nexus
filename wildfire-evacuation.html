Wildfire Threat & Evacuation Roadmap

Reduce ignition risk before the season, leave before conditions close your options, and return only when authorities say it is safe.

The life-safety strategy is early evacuation

Defensible space and home hardening may reduce structure ignition and help firefighters work. They do not make a property fireproof or turn it into a refuge. When authorities tell you to evacuate, leave immediately. Earlier departure may be appropriate when smoke, traffic, nighttime travel, animals, disability, or medical needs will slow evacuation.

Immediate threat: call 911, give your exact location, and follow local emergency instructions. Do not remain to defend property or retrieve optional belongings.

Five-stage decision roadmap

1 · Ready

Before fire season

Alerts, evacuation zones, routes, household roles, transportation, defensible space, documents, and practiced loading.

2 · Set

Dangerous fire weather

Monitor officials, charge devices, fuel vehicles, load essentials, and put every person and animal on a short departure clock.

3 · Leave

Fire or evacuation nearby

Use the safest open route directed by authorities. Do not wait for visible flames or for every device to receive the same alert.

4 · Emergency

Route unavailable

Call 911 with precise location, people, medical and mobility needs, conditions, and available shelter. Follow dispatcher instructions.

5 · Return

After official clearance

Expect hot material, utilities, falling trees, unsafe water, unstable structures, and later debris flows or flooding.

Before fire season

  • Register for alerts: identify the local systems used for evacuation notices and keep a battery-powered information source.
  • Know your zone: record its official name or number and where to verify current status.
  • Map several routes: drive them and note gates, bridges, narrow roads, seasonal closures, fuel stops, and destinations outside the hazard area.
  • Set early triggers: account for loading time, traffic, smoke sensitivity, limited road access, animals, disability, and medical needs.
  • Prepare transportation: keep vehicles maintained and fueled or charged; park facing the exit without blocking responders.
  • Plan reunification: choose an out-of-area contact and two meeting places. Never have family drive back into an evacuation area.
  • Protect records: photograph property, review insurance, and keep identification, prescriptions, and essential documents portable.

Defensible space and home hardening

Follow the responsible fire authority’s rules and inspection program. Distances vary by jurisdiction, slope, parcel, ecosystem, and current law. A useful planning model is:

  • Immediate area, about 0–5 feet: reduce ember ignition beside structures; clear roofs and gutters and address combustible debris, vents, decks, fences, furniture, and stored material.
  • Intermediate area, about 5–30 feet: remove dead vegetation, separate fuels, and break paths to decks, windows, eaves, and outbuildings.
  • Extended area, about 30–100 feet: reduce fuel continuity and accumulation while following ecological, erosion, permitting, and property-line requirements.
Local rules control. California uses Zone 0, 1, and 2 terminology, but Zone 0 requirements have been undergoing rulemaking. Confirm current effective rules before altering vegetation or structures.

Prioritize ember entry points, roofs, siding, windows, vents, decks, and combustible fence connections. Do not damage protected habitat, destabilize slopes, or clear beyond your authority.

When wildfire threatens

  • Monitor local emergency management, fire authorities, evacuation maps, closures, and NWS alerts. A Red Flag Warning describes dangerous fire weather; it is not itself a local evacuation order.
  • Charge communication and medical devices, load go-kits, attach trailers early, and bring pets into carriers. Last-minute animal loading may become impossible.
  • If time and officials permit, close windows and doors and move lightweight combustibles from structures. Never delay departure for property preparation.
  • Leave immediately when ordered. Use the open route directed by authorities even when it is not your normal route.
  • Drive predictably with headlights on and windows closed. Watch for responders, evacuees, animals, falling material, and stopped traffic.
  • Do not enter closed roads, use unofficial shortcuts, drive through active flame, stop on dry vegetation, or return for belongings.

Riparian areas are not automatic refuge

A creek, river, wetland, drainage, or green corridor may look safe because water is nearby. It can still be dangerous and should not replace an official evacuation destination.

  • Dense vegetation, dead material, and canopy can carry fire and produce intense smoke.
  • Low terrain may collect smoke; narrow canyons and drainages may channel wind and fire.
  • Banks, fences, mud, limited crossings, falling trees, and single-lane access can remove escape options.
  • Entering water adds drowning, current, cold, and entrapment hazards.
  • After fire, later rain can create flash floods, erosion, and debris flows.

Never assume a blue map feature or proximity to water is a wildfire safety zone.

Adaptive and medical evacuation

  • Contact emergency management before the season if evacuation requires accessible transport, transfers, electricity, refrigeration, oxygen, interpretation, or caregiver support.
  • Leave earlier when loading, medication timing, mobility, sensory needs, smoke sensitivity, or congestion can extend departure.
  • Keep medications, prescriptions, communication cards, chargers, batteries, mobility tools, prosthetic supplies, and equipment instructions together.
  • Confirm destinations accept service animals, pets, mobility equipment, and powered medical devices.
  • Assign and practice roles. Do not depend on one caregiver, vehicle, route, or alert method.

If evacuation becomes impossible

There is no universal last-resort location. Fire, construction, vehicles, fuels, wind, and terrain vary too much for a webpage to choose a survivable option.
  1. Call 911 with coordinates or address, people, injuries, medical and mobility needs, visible fire and smoke, and structures or vehicles available.
  2. Follow dispatcher and responder instructions and tell them before changing location.
  3. Do not improvise a route through flames or dense smoke, shelter beneath vegetation, or assume a creek is safe.
  4. Make the location visible when that does not increase exposure; keep communication devices available and conserve battery.

Return and recovery

  • Return only after authorities reopen the area; clearance does not guarantee every parcel or utility is safe.
  • Avoid hot ash, smoldering roots, unstable trees and structures, downed lines, leaking fuel, and damaged slopes.
  • Confirm whether water is safe and follow boil, do-not-drink, or do-not-use notices exactly.
  • Photograph damage before cleanup when safe and follow insurer and local disposal guidance.
  • Monitor rain forecasts and debris-flow or flash-flood warnings long after the fire.

A 30-minute departure drill

  1. Announce a fictional evacuation warning and start a timer without pre-positioning equipment.
  2. Dress, gather people and animals, retrieve medications and go-kits, and load mobility and communication equipment.
  3. Confirm the evacuation zone, official source, two routes, destination, and out-of-area contact.
  4. Load without blocking sight lines, exits, or critical supplies. Never idle a vehicle in an enclosed space.
  5. Stop when everyone is secured and ready. Record delays and single points of failure, then correct and repeat.
